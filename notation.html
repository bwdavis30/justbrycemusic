Notation

Before you are able to begin playing music, it is important that you first understand how to formally read the music. While we will mainly be focused on drum notation rather than the more notable struture organization of pitch-based clefs, it is important to understand all structures since pitch-based clefs define the basics of music structure. Music is organized in many different ways, and each of these structures are defined by the pitch in which the instrument plays. While there are a multitude of types of clefs, we will primarily focus on the shared aspects of each as well as slightly uncovering the most popular clefs - the treble clef and the bass clef.

The Clef System

In percussion, some of the most common clefs that are introduced in pitch-based percussion, such as the various keyboards percussion has to offer, is the treble and bass clef. The treble clef is for higher-pitched instruments while the bass clef is for the lower-pitched instruments. Though, since we are focused primarily on rudimental drumming, we only need to know that the clef structure system was only made to create an easier and universal way to read music without playing notes based on numbers - the hertz system. With this new structure, musicians were provided the ability to read music much more successfully with defined designs to understand the note, the length, and the volume.

The clef system is organized by five horizontal lines with each note being placed upon those lines to represent a different pitch. Once again, we are not concerned with the pitches in this lesson, but instead what the different notes mean. First, we will start with the root of the notes - a quarter note. This note is a filled in oval with a line on it's left side that stands alone. This note is commonly seen in rudimental drumming music, and serves as the length of a single beat. In rudimental drumming, we will almost never see anything longer than this note considering rudimental drums cannot be elongated for more than a single beat from their shortness in sound. Though, faster beats are written from being joined together with a line or defined with a tail at the end of the line. The more joint lines or tails, the higher the subdivision. One line is an eighth-note, two being sixteenth-note, four being thirty-second-note, and the pattern continues. However, the three subdivisions that are defined are the most common in drum notation.

Drum Notation

As more sophisticated percussion instruments were invented, so was the drum notation. This is essentially a clef system that is defined for drums. The presentation of these, such as how many lines are present, can massively vary depending on the composer. However, drum notation is always defined at the very beginning with two thick lines that resemble a pause button. While it is easier to understand in the aspect of not having to focus on pitch, there are much more details to be uncovered when reading music written in drum notation.

It is important to note that many of these details to be discussed are common to drum notation, but they are not unique to drum notation. Instead, many of these appear in pitch-based clef systems as well, but are discovered in this loesson considering their importance in reading music for rudimental drumming. The first thing to learn are time signatures. These are the two stacked numbers beside the clef notation (in this case the pause button) where the bottom number indicates the duration of each beat, and the top number indicates how many beats are in each measure, which is the short box-like divisions that are present all around the full piece. For example, 4/4 is one of the most common time signatures, which means each beat is equal to a quarter note and each beasure contains 4 notes.
